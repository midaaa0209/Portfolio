Welcome to the museum

The People of the Lake

The Maranao are a people associated with the Lake Lanao region of Mindanao, Philippines. The name Maranao (also spelled Meranaw) is commonly translated as "people of the lake".

This is a living culture, not something that belongs only to the past. It includes music, weaving, carving, architecture, stories, food, language and everyday community life, and it cannot be reduced to a stereotype. No single exhibit can speak for every Maranao person, so each hall shares selected, documented parts of a culture that is rich and diverse.

Section 1

Kulintang: The Sound of the Maranao

The kulintang is a row of small, tuned, knobbed gongs laid on a frame. Musical traditions differ from community to community in Mindanao and the southern Philippines, so this exhibit describes only the general picture.

Section 3

Visual Culture: Okir and Metalwork

Okir is a distinctive artistic tradition of Maranao visual culture: flowing, plant-like and geometric forms carved, painted and woven into many kinds of objects and buildings.

    Section 4

    Royal Architecture: The Torogan

    The torogan is a traditional Maranao house of a chief or noble family. It is a symbol of status and was also a gathering place for the community. Not every Maranao family lived in a torogan, and today's households live in many kinds of homes.

    What to look for

    • Raised on large wooden columns
    • Built without nails, using fitted joints and fiber lashings
    • Panolong: carved beam ends that look like the prow of a boat, often showing the niyaga, a mythical sea serpent
    • Okir carving on posts and beams, often painted in bright colors
    • A large open main hall with a thatched roof

    Protecting heritage

    Few early torogan remain, and many have been replaced by newer houses. The Kawayan Torogan in Marantao, Lanao del Sur was declared a National Cultural Treasure by the National Museum in 2008. Other torogan, such as the Dayawan Torogan in Marawi and the Laguindab Torogan in Ganassi, need major funds for rehabilitation.

    Protecting these buildings means protecting the skills, stories and community memories connected to them.
